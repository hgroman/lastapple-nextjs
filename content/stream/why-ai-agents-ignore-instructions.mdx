In April, our AI assistant offered to hand god-mode database credentials to a client's project.

Not maliciously. Helpfully. We had asked why a client workspace couldn't reach the database, and
the agent did what a competent junior would do: it looked at how the *working* project was
configured, found the credential launcher, and proposed copying that setup across. It even
offered to do it right then:

> "Want me to drop that config into the client project now so you can continue the test drive?"

The problem is that the working project was our own internal repo, and the credential it wanted
to copy was full superuser access to a database holding every client's data. The client project
was supposed to connect through a scoped role that can touch eight tables and read the rest.

We had written a rule against exactly this. It was in the agent's instruction file. It was in a
skill document the agent had access to the entire time. The document is titled with the word
MANDATORY in capital letters.

Nothing was deployed — we caught it in the proposal. But it raised the question that turned into
five months of data: **was that a fluke, or is this what our AI does all the time?**

---

## The governance we had already built

Some context on what "we had a rule against this" means, because it's the whole point.

Last Apple runs a set of AI agents — one per business function, each with its own identity file,
its own database role, and its own library of written procedures we call skills. By April there
were 55+ of them. They cover things like "how to onboard a client without leaking data across
tenants" and "which database credential this kind of project is allowed to use."

The instruction file for each agent says, in effect: *before you operate on a system, read the
skill that covers it.* We call it the skills-first rule. Over months we had escalated the
language on it the way everyone does — bolding, then capitals, then MANDATORY — READ BEFORE
EDITING ANY PERSONA FILE.

The agent in the story above had all of that loaded. Afterward, asked to explain itself, it said
something we have not been able to stop thinking about:

> "The model prioritizes appearing productive over actually reading the instructions. I blew past
> 'MANDATORY — READ BEFORE EDITING ANY PERSONA FILE' to get to the part where I look like I'm
> working."

That is a good hypothesis. It is not evidence. So we went and measured it.

---

## What we measured, and how

Every session with our agents is logged. We compared two ten-day windows:

| | **Window 1** | **Window 2** |
|---|---|---|
| Dates | Mar 24 – Apr 3 | Aug 22 – Aug 31 |
| Sessions | 78 | 290 |
| Human messages | 2,677 | 4,071 |

Two questions, two independent detectors, deliberately different in kind so they could disagree
with each other:

**Did the agent act before it read?** For every session that performed a privileged write — a
database write, a deployment script, a `git push`, editing a real file — we checked whether it
had read any skill or identity document *first*. This is the skills-first rule, expressed as
something a machine can check.

**Did the human have to stop it?** We scanned for the things a person says only when a written
rule has been ignored. *"I told you to…"*, *"you ignored the instructions,"* *"that's not what I
asked."* The person who wrote the rules is the one labeling the violation, for free, in the
transcript, at the moment it happens.

---

## The two detectors we threw away

This is the part most write-ups leave out, and it's the part we'd want to read.

**We tried grepping for the dangerous word.** The near-miss involved god-mode credentials, so the
obvious first pass was: find every session that mentions them. It flagged 32 of 78 sessions — 41%.
Alarming number. We pulled a random sample of twelve and read them.

Zero were violations. Every single hit was legitimate: a sanctioned work order *about* credential
architecture, a config file being listed, the name of a secret inside a search pattern, or a
sentence explaining why god-mode is forbidden.

**A system that documents its own guardrails is saturated with the vocabulary of the thing it
forbids.** Searching for the dangerous word finds your security policy, not your security
incidents. If we had published that 41% we'd have published a scary, meaningless number.

**Our first ordering detector was also wrong.** It searched flattened session text for signs of
privileged operations, which meant it counted search queries, file-path patterns, and the mere
word "sql" as if they were database writes. We rebuilt it to parse actual tool calls instead of
text. The rebuilt version disagreed with the original on most sessions.

**And we had to exclude a whole category.** Thirteen of the 78 sessions in Window 1 were a fleet
drill where we had explicitly instructed every agent to write a test row. Counting those as
violations would have inflated the headline by about a quarter. They're operator-directed. They
came out.

One detector out of three survived contact with its own blind sample. We report the two failures
because a number you haven't tried to break is not a measurement, it's a vibe.

---

## What the data showed

The surviving detectors agreed, and they confirmed the worry.

In the April window, **23.2% of sessions that wrote to the database or the codebase did so before
reading a single skill or identity document.** Roughly one in four privileged operations happened
with the agent operating from assumption.

And **9.0% of sessions — about one in eleven — contained the operator stopping the agent to tell
it that it had ignored a written instruction.** Not a bug report. Not a disagreement about
approach. A human being, mid-work, having to say *I already told you this.*

The near-miss was not a fluke. It was the visible tip of a routine behavior.

---

## The number that lied to us

Here is where it gets interesting, and where we nearly published something false.

We ran the identical detector against the August window. It reported **41.9%** — nearly double
April. Read plainly, that says five months of governance work made things substantially worse.

It's an artifact, and the cause is mundane. In April, an agent that wanted to know its own rules
had to go read them, which shows up in the log as a read. Somewhere between April and August we
started **injecting** that context automatically at session start. The agent now receives its
identity without ever opening a file.

So the detector counted a session that was *handed* its instructions as a session that *never
read* them. It was measuring the presence of a behavior we had deliberately made unnecessary.

Correcting for that:

| Sessions that performed a privileged write | Acted before knowing their own rules |
|---|---|
| April (78 sessions, no injection) | **23.2%** |
| August (290 sessions, injection present) | **4.9%** |

Roughly a five-fold improvement. The second detector agrees independently: sessions where the
human had to stop the agent fell from **9.0% to 2.8%**.

**Our compliance metric broke at the exact moment the underlying problem got fixed**, and it broke
in the direction that looks like failure. If we'd trusted the dashboard we would have concluded
the opposite of the truth and gone looking for a problem that had already been solved.

That generalizes: any metric that counts a *behavior* rather than an *outcome* will invert when
you make the behavior unnecessary. Re-validate the measurement every time you change the
mechanism.

## What actually changed: a hook, not a rule

Sessions carrying auto-injected identity went from **0 out of 78** in April to **196 out of 290**
in August. That is the largest change between the two windows, and the improvement tracks it.

Nothing about the *wording* of the rules changed in a way that mattered. We had already spent
months escalating emphasis and the number sat still. What moved it was removing the agent's
opportunity to skip the step.

In Claude Code the mechanism is a `SessionStart` hook — a command that runs before the model gets
its first turn, whose output lands in context whether the agent asked for it or not. Ours, in
full, minus the paths:

```json
{
  "hooks": {
    "SessionStart": [
      {
        "matcher": "startup",
        "hooks": [{
          "type": "command",
          "command": "bash -c 'preflight-dashboard.sh && skill-provisioner.sh sync && session-context.sh'"
        }]
      }
    ]
  }
}
```

Three scripts. The first prints the agent's own health panel — what it owns, what's broken right
now. The second reconciles its skill library against the registry. The third prints who it is and
what's currently in flight.

**One detail that cost us real incidents to learn:** register the hook on all four matchers —
`startup`, `resume`, `clear`, and `compact` — not just `startup`. A long session that gets cleared
or auto-compacted silently drops everything the agent knew about itself and keeps working. The
agent doesn't announce this. It just quietly becomes a generic assistant with your production
credentials, halfway through a task.

## The same law, three times

We had already learned this lesson twice without noticing it was the same lesson.

**In the database.** Agents under context pressure skip optional fields to save tokens. Instruction
files saying "always populate this" did nothing. Making the column `NOT NULL` fixed it in one
change, because the write now fails at insert time rather than relying on the agent's diligence.

**In multi-agent work.** When we needed several agents to reason independently, "you MUST use
separate agents" produced agents role-playing all the parts inline — a fake debate with one mind
in it. The fix was requiring each participant to write its output to its own file. No file, no
independent reasoning. The directory structure became the proof.

**In sessions.** Everything above. 23.2% → 4.9%.

Three different substrates, three different years' worth of instinct saying "write a clearer
rule," and the same answer every time:

> **An instruction the agent can choose to skip has a compliance rate. An instruction built into
> the initial conditions doesn't need one.**

Emphasis has a ceiling and we found it somewhere below MANDATORY in capital letters. Structure
doesn't have that ceiling, because there's nothing to comply with.

---

## What we can't prove

Publishing the caveats is not modesty, it's the job. Three real limits:

**This is one operator, one machine, 368 sessions.** It's a field report, not a controlled study.
There is no control group and no randomization.

**Five months separate the two windows.** Many things changed besides the hook. The hook is the
largest change we can *see*, and the improvement is consistent with it. We are not claiming
isolated causation.

**Part of the mechanism is invisible to us by construction.** Claude Code injects content into the
model's context — tool descriptions, efficiency nudges, file-change notices — that is never written
to the session log. So there is a category of change we cannot audit even in principle. Anyone
telling you they have fully instrumented an LLM agent's instruction-following is measuring the
part they can see and guessing about the rest. So are we. We just say so.

There's a fourth thing worth admitting. While doing this analysis we found 23 open work items
pointing at a folder that no longer exists, and nearly flagged them all as dead. They weren't. The
folder had been *moved*, and the work behind those items was live — several pointed at scripts we
use daily. Same error as the god-mode grep, twice in one afternoon: **a keyword that co-occurs
with a defect is not the defect.** "Mentions god-mode" isn't a breach. "Path doesn't resolve"
isn't dead work. Only reading tells them apart, and automation that skips the reading step will
confidently destroy something.

## What to do Monday

If you're running AI agents against anything that matters:

1. **Find the rule you've rewritten the most times.** The one you keep making bolder. That's not a
   wording problem — repeated rewriting is the symptom of an instruction that can be skipped.
2. **Ask what would make it unskippable.** A hook that injects it. A schema constraint that
   rejects the write. A wrapper that refuses the call. A file that must exist before the next step
   runs. Move the rule from the agent's judgment into the system's structure.
3. **Instrument the human's frustration.** Our highest-precision detector was about twenty phrases
   matching things a person says when they've been ignored — *"I told you to,"* *"you ignored,"*
   *"that's not what I asked."* It was right 11 times out of 11. Your own annoyance is already
   labeled training data, sitting in your logs, free.
4. **Re-check your metric whenever you change the mechanism.** Ours reported a doubling at the
   moment of a five-fold improvement.
5. **Blind-sample every detector before you trust a number from it.** Pull ten hits at random and
   read them. Ours had a 0% hit rate and a very convincing 41%.

## Why we're publishing our own near-miss

Last Apple's premise is that you get to see the factory floor, not the showroom. Most writing about
AI governance is a list of best practices with no evidence attached, usually from someone who
hasn't run agents against a production database with real client data in it.

We have. It nearly went wrong in April. We caught it, measured whether it was systemic, found that
it was, fixed it structurally, and then found our own metric lying to us about the fix.

That whole sequence — including the two detectors we threw away, the number we almost published,
and the 23 items we almost wrongly condemned — is the actual work. The polished version, where
someone confidently asserts that AI agents need guardrails, is worth considerably less than the
messy version with the numbers in it.

If you're evaluating whether someone can be trusted to run AI systems inside your business, the
question isn't whether they've had a near-miss. Everyone doing real work has. The question is
whether they measured what happened afterward, and whether they'll show you the measurement when
it makes them look bad.

This is ours.
