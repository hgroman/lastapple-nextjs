This is a band of sky, not a globe.

Seven frames, thirty degrees of yaw apart, stitched into one strip that covers
**238.7 degrees across and 48.7 degrees top to bottom** — about two-thirds of the
way around, and roughly a quarter of the way up. The horizon runs close to the
centre of the band. Drag it left and right and you run out of picture, because that
is where the camera stopped.

There is no floor and no ceiling in it. Nothing was filled in, blurred out, or
generated to close the gaps, so there is nothing to look at straight up or straight
down. A viewer that offers you those directions is offering you something the drone
never saw.

## What is in it

Westfield, in Brookfield Township, Tioga County, Pennsylvania, on 2 September 2026
at 19:38:00 in the evening. The drone was 155 metres above its launch point and the
ground there is 529 metres up, which puts the camera at **684 metres above sea
level** — about 2,244 feet — at 41.955061, -77.553538.

The sun was at **plus two-tenths of a degree**: not below the horizon, but as close
to it as makes no difference, and behind a deck of cloud, so none of its light
reaches the ground in this frame directly.

The orange is on the anvil. That thunderhead is tall enough to still be standing in
sunlight while everything underneath it has already gone blue, and it is the last
direct sun in the picture. Everything below the ridge line is lit by the sky, not by
the sun.

Shot on a DJI Mini 4K — a sub-250-gram consumer drone, not a cinema rig. Seven
frames at thirty-degree steps gave thirty-nine degrees of overlap at each seam,
which is what makes the stitch hold together across that much sky.

## The one that got away

Thirty-two seconds later the same view was gone:

![The same sweep 32 seconds later, with the land fallen almost entirely into black silhouette beneath the still-lit anvil cloud](/images/skies/tioga-180/tioga-1938b-2048.jpg)

Same position, same lens, same seven-frame sweep — trimmed to its own slightly
different rectangle, because the usable edge of a stitch moves when the light does.
The sun had dropped to **plus one-tenth of a degree** by 19:38:32, and the land is
**1.8 stops darker**,
and just under five per cent of the ground pixels have gone to flat black with
nothing recoverable in them. At that hour the exposure you get is the exposure the
sky hands you, and half a minute is enough to lose it.

It is kept here because it is honest about how narrow the window was, not because it
is a second photograph. The one above is the photograph.

## Why it is presented this way

The obvious move with a wide strip is to stretch it into a shape that fills a
screen. Doing that to this file would bend the horizon and invent sky at the top and
bottom that nobody flew for.

So the frame is 4.9 to 1 because the picture is 4.9 to 1, and the viewer's
projection is computed from the angular metadata written into the file itself rather
than typed in by hand to taste. If those two ever disagree, the site fails to build.
That is a deliberately annoying gate, and it is there because the tool that
originally packaged this panorama got exactly this wrong — it was written for full
spheres, and it labelled a partial band as a complete 360 and squashed it to fit.
The files here were tagged by hand instead.

A picture of a place should be checkable against the place.
