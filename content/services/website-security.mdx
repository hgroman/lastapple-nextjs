## What This Is

Two things get sold as website security. One is a monitoring plugin that emails you when a version number changes. The other is a person who arrives after something has already happened.

This is the second one, plus the part almost nobody buys: **the review that goes looking before there is an alert to react to.**

The through-line across every engagement below is the same. **The alert is rarely the most important thing on the site.** It is the thing that happened to be instrumented.

## When The Site Is Already Compromised

One engagement began the way most do — something looked slightly wrong. What the server actually held was a supply-chain compromise inherited from a previous developer.

The site was running cracked commercial plugins, pulled from one of the "GPL vault" resale sites that redistribute paid software with the licence checks removed. Three things came with them:

- **An active file-upload webshell.** Not a dormant artifact. A working remote channel for putting new files on the server.
- **A cracked page builder that sabotaged the genuine licence.** The client was paying for the real product, and the counterfeit copy was preventing it from activating — so the legitimate updates that would have closed known vulnerabilities never arrived.
- **Hidden remote-control channels that survived restores.** This is the part that matters commercially: the site could be rolled back and would come back compromised, which is exactly why the problem had persisted.

The same-day response was forensic triage, malware and backdoor removal, replacement of the counterfeit software with genuine licensed builds, full credential rotation, edge and firewall hardening, and a verified-clean backup taken at the end rather than assumed at the start.

## The Restore Point Is Part Of The Attack

The single most expensive mistake in a WordPress compromise is restoring to a backup taken after the intrusion and before the symptom.

On that engagement the obvious restore point was still infected. Establishing that — reading the attack chain backwards until the first known-clean state is identified, rather than picking the most recent backup that renders — is why the recovery held. A full integrity sweep across 24,939 files followed, so "clean" meant enumerated rather than believed.

**A restore that reinstates the backdoor is not a recovery. It is a delay with an invoice attached.**

## The Review That No Alert Triggered

A different engagement started from a low-grade automated warning about an outdated component. The warning was accurate and almost irrelevant.

The review it prompted found a **third-party access grant issued in 2020 that was still live** — and that had survived a full post-compromise credential rotation earlier that year, because the rotation's scope said "all credentials" and an external integration's standing grant was not in anybody's list. It also found **no two-factor authentication on any administrator account.**

Neither of those generates an email. Ever. A control that was never configured and a control that silently stopped working look identical from outside the server, and monitoring products are built to report change, not absence.

## A Clean Verdict Is A Deliverable

On another site we ran a multi-vector review — core file integrity against the official checksums, a malware and webshell sweep, and a cloaking test that compares what the server returns to a search crawler against what it returns to a browser, because a site can be serving one thing to Google and another to you.

**It came back clean.** Scanner: zero issues. That result was written up and delivered exactly as thoroughly as a bad one would have been, in one session, with a verdict the client could act on.

We say this plainly because the incentive runs the other way. A security vendor that never finds anything struggles to justify itself, and the temptation is to inflate a housekeeping item into a threat. A clean report you can trust is worth more than a long one you can't.

## The Control Was On. It Was Not Doing Anything.

A client's store was being hit with stolen cards. The team who owned the payment gateway had already applied exactly what they were asked to apply, and had applied it correctly — a velocity limit on attempts per hour, and a rule declining any transaction where the card security code did not match.

Both settings were on. Both were correct. Anyone who opened the panel and asked *"is fraud detection enabled?"* would have been told yes, and **the yes would have been true.**

The attack continued anyway.

The gateway's fraud suite also checks the billing address, and that filter was enabled too. What nobody had read was the row-by-row configuration underneath it. The filter was set to **decline** on structural failures — no billing address supplied, malformed data, a card issued outside the country. Every one of those was correctly set to reject.

And the response that means *the address was supplied and does not match* — which is precisely what a stolen card number with an invented billing address produces — was set to allow, and was being waved straight through to authorization.

So the filter was enabled. The filter was correctly described to the client. The filter was, for this specific attack, doing nothing at all.

**We read the per-code action rows and changed the one that mattered.** We also reset the rate threshold, which had been derived twice from the wrong window: the attack's per-hour peak was three attempts, so any threshold above that could never fire inside the one hour the filter actually evaluates. A threshold set from the span of an attack instead of the width of the filter's window is a control that is switched on and mathematically unable to trigger.

### Why we can say it worked

The store had been reopened once **before** that change, and the attack came back within hours — two fraudulent orders eight minutes apart, matching the earlier wave's signature exactly.

Reopened again with the row closed, the script returned within minutes and met working controls. Four weeks followed with **no fraudulent order at all, against eight clean ones.**

That is not proof, and we will not call it proof. A control that works and an attacker who moved on look identical from outside. But it is the closest thing to a controlled test an incident ever hands you: the same store, the same surface, one variable moved, and a documented precedent showing exactly what unfixed looked like.

The distinction we insist on: **the store going dark for a day is not a defence, and we never counted the quiet as evidence.** Silence while the payment gateways are switched off tells you about opportunity, not about protection. The window that counts is the one where the surface was demonstrably live, demonstrably still being attacked, and held.

### Defeated is not bypassed

One more thing that engagement settled, because it decides what you buy next.

The bot challenge on that checkout had not been skipped. It had been **beaten** — the requests were solving or farming tokens and passing it. Request timing separated the two cases at zero cost: the carding attempts took three to five seconds because they reached the gateway, while a request with no token comes back in under a second having never got there.

That matters commercially. **Bypassed** means a control was skipped, and the fix is to close the skip path. **Defeated** means the control ran, was satisfied, and the attacker met it — and the fix is a second independent layer, never a replacement for the first. Ripping out the bot check would have removed a working control and changed nothing.

The underlying gap was that once a request passed the bot check, there was no gateway-side control behind it. **One layer is not a defence. It is a single point of failure with good branding.**

## Forms And Bots: Layered, On Purpose

Contact forms are the cheapest way into a site's inbox, its CRM and its sender reputation.

We deploy **layered** defence — an invisible trap field plus a modern challenge widget, with rate limiting and content filtering behind them — on the working assumption that **any single challenge will eventually be defeated.** We don't publish a claim about which vendor's challenge is currently strongest, because that changes faster than a service page does. The design principle is what survives: no single control carries the whole load.

The other half is knowing that the same-looking symptom can be two different threats. A form flooded by a scripted bot and a form used by a human operation are not the same problem and do not take the same fix, and choosing the lighter control when the lighter control is sufficient is part of the work.

## What We Decline

**We do not sell a monitoring subscription as security.** Monitoring is a smoke alarm. It is worth having and it is not a fire department.

**We do not publish our incident specifics with client names attached.** Everything above is anonymized. If your engagement goes badly, it does not become a case study — and neither does anyone else's without a signed conversation first.

**We will not count silence as a defence.** If your attacker went quiet while the surface was switched off, the honest word is *untested*, not *held* — and we will say so even when the reassuring version is right there and easier to sell. What we will claim is what we changed, and what happened when the surface went back up.

## Who This Isn't For

**Not for you if you want a compliance certificate.** We are not an auditor and we will not sign an attestation. If the requirement is a badge for a procurement form, buy the badge.

**Not for you if the answer has to be "you're fine."** Reviews sometimes find that the most serious problem is one nobody had noticed and nobody had budgeted for. If that is unwelcome news rather than useful news, this engagement will be uncomfortable.
