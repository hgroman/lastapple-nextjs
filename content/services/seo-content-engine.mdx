## What This Is

Most agency content is a press release with the vendor's adjectives left in. Someone reads the announcement email, rewrites it warmer, adds a keyword, and ships.

We do the unglamorous version: **we go to the vendor's own published documentation, check what the announcement actually claims, and correct it before it reaches your customers.**

That is slower. It is also the only version that survives a reader who knows the product.

## Straight to the Source

There is usually a chain between a product and the article about it: the manufacturer publishes, a distributor summarizes, a partner emails, an agency rewrites the email. Every hop loses fidelity and nobody checks backwards.

We skip the chain. For a healthcare technology client in medical speech recognition, our content is written against **the manufacturer's own published release notes** — not the partner announcement, not the distributor's help center, not a third-party roundup.

What that catches, in practice:

**A false architecture claim, intercepted.** A widely circulating third-party summary asserted a major release "is based on a new architecture." Checked against the manufacturer's documentation, it wasn't. That claim never reached the client's site, and it is still out there on other people's.

**A factual error caught inside our own draft.** A post had been sitting in client review, written from a partner email because no public release notes existed yet. When the manufacturer published, we re-checked — and the draft asserted a capability the release notes did not list, while the *previous* version's notes promised exactly that. We reported both documents and told the reader to verify in their own environment rather than picking a side and being confidently wrong.

**A deadline nobody had published.** One announcement contained no feature list at all, and the real release notes sat behind a login we had no credential for. There was no honest way to write a conventional "what's new" post — so we didn't write one. Verification instead surfaced something more valuable than a feature list: a hard, dated cut-off after which older builds simply stop working, because the cloud service blocks them. That consequence appeared nowhere in the vendor's own announcement. It became the spine of the page.

That last one is the whole philosophy. **The post we refused to pad became the most useful page in the engagement.**

## Hub and Spoke, With a Gate

Content that ranks is not a pile of posts. It is a structure: **pillar pages that hold the durable commercial claims, and spokes** — release notes, updates, comparisons — that drive intent traffic into them.

The part most people skip is the gate.

A spoke sends motivated readers into a pillar page. If that pillar's central promise is wrong or stale, the spoke is an amplifier for a mistake. So on our engagements, **verifying the pillar's core claim is a blocking task on the spoke's publication.** We have held posts specifically because a pillar page was carrying a supported-platforms list that no longer matched what the platform vendor documented.

A page that says "currently integrates with eleven platforms" inside FAQ schema is not marketing copy. It is a structured claim that Google reads and a buyer acts on. It gets checked with the platform owner directly, not inferred from a distributor's help pages.

## What E-E-A-T Actually Requires

Google's guidance on Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust gets treated as an author-bio problem — add a headshot, add credentials, done.

We treat it as an evidence problem. First-hand experience means the page demonstrably knows things the press release doesn't: prerequisites, version-specific limits, regional availability, the configuration rule that breaks an upgrade, how an administrator reads the current version across an estate. That is what a practitioner actually searches for, and it is what a summarizer cannot produce.

One rewrite in this engagement went from five bullets to **twenty-two documented features with per-feature regional limits** — not to hit a word count, but because that was what the primary source actually contained and the summary had dropped.

## The Technical Half

Content only works if the crawler agrees. Same hands, same engagement:

- **Sitemap hygiene** — we have removed noindexed taxonomy URLs from a client sitemap that were spending crawl budget to be ignored
- **Index-coverage readback** — verifying what Google actually did with a page, via the Search Console URL Inspection API, rather than trusting a submission receipt
- **Domain portfolio audits** — finding the duplicate hostnames, stray legacy sitemaps and crawlable staging subdomains that quietly compete with the site you're optimizing
- **Render verification on the live URL** after cache flush, because a green build is not evidence that anything reached a reader

We publish our own failures in this area too. The [Stream](/stream) has the audit where our own site was found publishing into a void with dead analytics for seven months.

## Who This Isn't For

**Not for you if you want volume.** This is expensive per post and the output is lower. If the plan is forty articles a month, we are the wrong shop and will say so.

**Not for you if the product has no primary source.** This method needs a manufacturer, a standards body, a regulator or a dataset to verify against. For a product whose only documentation is its own marketing, there is nothing to check and the advantage disappears.

**Not for you if "the vendor said so" is good enough.** The entire value here is that we treat the vendor's announcement as a claim rather than a fact. If that reads as friction rather than diligence, the engagement will be frustrating for both of us.

**Not for you if you need it live this week.** Verification takes as long as the source takes to answer.
