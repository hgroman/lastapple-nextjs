## What This Is

Four related things, sold together because they are the same muscle: **someone reads a large amount of material carefully and tells you the part that changes your decision.**

Most "research" deliverables are a summary. A summary tells you what was said. What you actually need is what is *load-bearing* — the assumption that will not survive a hard question in the room.

## Six Models, One Question

The method: take a real question, write one detailed prompt, submit it **identically** to six independent AI models, collect the full unedited responses, and synthesize.

The synthesis has four parts, always: **where they agree, where they diverge, what they all missed, and what to do about it.**

Convergence is the useful signal. One model's confident answer is one model's confident answer. When six independently reach the same objection, that objection is going to come up in your meeting.

### What it looked like on a real proposal

A client brought a financial proposal to validate before it went in front of a health system's leadership: a defined patient cohort, an intervention, a projected utilization reduction, and a savings range.

We pulled thirteen assumptions out of it and put a ten-section prompt to all six models. Four findings came back:

- **Where all six agreed:** the unit costs and utilization rates were solid — in fact *conservative* against real figures. The core pressure argument was real. That is worth knowing before you defend it.
- **Where all six pushed back:** the single projected reduction that the whole savings case rested on was the weakest assumption in the document. Two randomised controlled trials on comparable cohorts had tested *more intensive* versions of the same intervention and found no significant effect at twelve months. All six models landed on it independently. The revised range was materially lower — and defensible.
- **A factual error, caught before the room:** a published benchmark figure in the proposal was wrong by several points. The argument it supported still held, but it *overstated the urgency* — the kind of error that costs you the room's trust on a slide you did not need.
- **A financial play nobody in the document had considered:** a reimbursement route that could have turned a cost line into billable revenue.

**Three of those four made the proposal weaker and better.** That is what the deliverable is for. A research pass that only confirms what you brought is a research pass you did not need.

## Reading Rivals For the Claim, Not the Feature

Competitive intelligence goes wrong when it becomes a feature-comparison spreadsheet nobody reads twice.

What matters is **what a competitor is claiming**, because the claim names the lane they are fighting for — and the lanes nobody is claiming are the opportunity. We read for the sentence they lead with, the objection they are pre-empting, and the price they are anchoring, then ask one question of each: *do we have an answer to this?*

That question has three honest outcomes and we will give you whichever is true: yes and here is the proof; no and here is what it would take; or **no, and this lane should be conceded rather than bluffed.** The third one is the most useful and almost nobody says it.

We also read the market, not just the rivals — industry shifts, technology changes, buyer behaviour — because the thing that displaces you is usually not on your competitor list.

## The Interpretive Layer On Your Own Data

**We do not just send you reports. We read them, connect them, and tell you what they mean before you have to ask.**

An automated dashboard tells you a number moved. It cannot tell you that the movement is a ratio artifact, or that a rise in one metric is being caused by something you should stop doing, or that the account holding your growth is the one that looks flattest.

That is a person's job, and it is the difference between reporting and analytics.

## Who This Isn't For

**Not for you if you want the answer you already have.** The value in this is the finding that costs you something. If a research pass that weakens your own proposal is unwelcome, this will be an expensive way to be annoyed.

**Not for you if the deliverable has to be a deck.** We write findings, not presentations, and the finding is usually shorter and more uncomfortable than a deck.

**Not for you if there is nothing to verify against.** This method needs a documented world — regulators, standards bodies, published trials, primary vendor documentation. For a question whose only source is opinion, six models will confidently give you six opinions and we will have sold you nothing.
